Definitions

Every term the design relies on, in plain English.

The design

What the experiment is made of.

Estimand
The quantity you want to know, written down as a specific number before you run anything. Distinct from the estimate (the number you get) and the estimator (the recipe for computing it). Naming it in advance is what stops you fishing.
Here: connected − severed, the difference in convergence between agents who can see each other and agents who cannot.
ConvergenceC = σ(round 0) − σ(round 3)
How much the spread of answers shrank over the three rounds of revision. Measured as a reduction in dispersion, so it has a defined value at every round and gives a trajectory rather than a single endpoint.
Baseline homogeneity
The agreement that is there before anyone speaks. Twenty-four copies of one model, given one prompt, produce similar answers having never exchanged a message. This is the term the literature measures and attributes to interaction.
Transmitted convergence
The agreement that interaction actually added. Observed convergence is the sum of the two, and no published study separates them.
Severed arm
Agents cannot see each other at all. They still revise three times, alone. This measures the floor — how much they converge purely from being the same model with the same prompt. The prompt is held byte-identical to the connected arm so that only the channel differs.
Connected arm
Agents see the peers they are wired to. Floor plus whatever talking adds.
Sham arm
Agents are shown real model-written messages harvested from independent severed runs. The text is genuine; nobody is responding to them. They get the experience of being talked to with no conversation happening.
From sham surgery — make the incision, do nothing else. The social-science ancestor is Crutchfield's 1955 booth experiment, where participants believed they were seeing others' answers that the experimenter had invented.
Contrarian arm
The same mechanism as sham, but messages are drawn from the unusual tail of the severed corpus — positions that cut against what the model normally says. The directional test: can influence push against a prior, or only amplify it?
Yoked control
A control that receives exactly the same inputs as the treated subject — same content, same order — with the causal link cut. Any difference is about the link, not the stimulus.
From animal learning, where pairs were yoked so one animal controlled a shock and the other received identical shocks it could not control. Harvesting sham and contrarian from the same corpus makes them style-matched by construction: the only difference is where in the distribution the message sits.
Echo test
The sham comparison. If agents fed messages nobody is responding to converge nearly as much as agents in real conversation, then most of what the field calls influence is a model hearing its own distribution returned to it.
Sham ratio
Sham's convergence as a percentage of connected's. 85% means the fake conversation did 85% of the work of the real one. Under pure averaging it runs 62–95% depending on assumptions — which is why the original H2 was not a falsifiable test.
Wiring
Among connected agents, who can see whom. Four levels: severed, sparse-random, clustered, dense. The contrast that matters is sparse-random against clustered at matched density — same number of connections, different redundancy.
Log signature
An observable pattern in the interaction transcript that reveals which process produced it, without needing to know the arm. The experiment can decompose convergence only because the channel is controlled; deployed systems leave only a transcript. Four candidates: adoption-curve shape, lead–lag consistency, peer-referencing rate, independence ratio.

Parameters

Three of these are things the agents do and you can only measure. One is a decision you make. That distinction is what makes the sensitivity analysis actionable.

Dispersion floorσ₀
How spread out the answers are before anyone sees anyone, measured on logs. Humans in Lorenz et al. sat at about 1.95; agents at a fixed temperature will be far tighter. Called the floor because it is the convergence you get for free. If it is near zero there is nothing left for interaction to explain, and the design fails for a measurement reason rather than a substantive one.
Set by: the agents. Nudged by item choice and temperature, then measured.
Updating weightw
How far an agent moves toward what it was shown. w = 0 means it ignores peers entirely; w = 1 means it abandons its own answer. A property of how the model behaves.
Set by: the agents. Only observable once they can see each other, so estimating it needs a small connected pilot.
Decoding noiseε
Ask the same agent the same question twice and the answer differs, because generation is stochastic. Driven largely by temperature.
Set by: the agents, via temperature.
Messages shownK
How many harvested messages each agent is fed in the sham and contrarian arms. The feed is the mean of K draws, so its variance is σ₀²/K — averaging more messages makes the target tighter and squeezes agents together for reasons unrelated to influence. At K = 1 the sham ratio falls to 15%; at K = 8 it is 79%.
Set by: you. A design choice, which is why it gets pre-registered rather than left at its default.
Group size and roundsg = 24, R = 3
g = 24 keeps the clustered-versus-random clustering gap wide; at g = 12 the contrast collapses. R = 3 matches Becker et al.'s round count so the human benchmark stays comparable — but the published multi-agent work runs 200 to 1,000 rounds, so three rounds measures the opening of the trajectory, not where it settles.

Statistics and method

σlog
The standard deviation of log estimates across agents, computed per round. Logs because estimates of real quantities span orders of magnitude and being 10× too high should count the same as 10× too low. On raw estimates the sample mean is not a measure of the crowd, it is a measure of its most extreme member — on one item the arithmetic mean sits 22× above the geometric mean.
DeGroot updating
Each round, replace your opinion with a weighted average of the opinions you can see, including your own. No persuasion, no reading, just arithmetic. The standard weakest-possible model of social learning (DeGroot, 1974).
Used here as a null to beat, not a prediction. If a hypothesis is satisfied by a system that does nothing but average, the hypothesis was not testing a social process.
A null to beat
A prediction is something you hope to confirm; a null is something you hope to exceed. Three are in play: the shuffled-condition permutation null, the averaging-implied null from DeGroot updating, and the recursive self-conditioning floor the severed arm measures. They fail differently — one asks whether the labels matter, one whether arithmetic explains it, one whether a peer was needed at all.
Equivalence testingTOST
A non-significant result does not mean no effect; it is also what low power looks like. Two one-sided tests let you pre-specify the smallest effect you would care about and show the true effect is reliably smaller than that bound — positively demonstrating negligibility rather than failing to find something.
Lakens (2017). Load-bearing here: "the transmitted component is near zero" is the headline claim and cannot be made without it.
Autocorrelation-robust errors
Ordinary regression assumes independent observations. In a time series they are not — hour 50 resembles hour 49 — so standard errors come out too small and noise looks like signal. Newey–West recomputes them allowing nearby observations to correlate.
Newey & West (1987). In the Moltbook analysis two specifications looked significant and both died under it.
False discovery rate
Run twenty tests at p < 0.05 and expect one false positive by chance. Benjamini– Hochberg controls the share of your significant results that are false, rather than the chance of any false positive at all — less brutal than Bonferroni. One pre-registered confirmatory measure, three exploratory.
Benjamini & Hochberg (1995).
ODD protocol
Overview, Design concepts, Details — a fixed documentation structure for agent-based models, so two simulations can be compared on substance rather than write-up. Covers entities, state variables, scheduling, stochasticity, initialisation and submodels.
Grimm et al. (2006), updated 2010.
Simple vs complex contagion
Simple contagion needs one exposure and travels on sparse, far-reaching ties. Complex contagion needs several independent exposures and travels on clustered, redundant ones. The prediction: a false claim spreads as simple contagion, while revising a claim an agent has already committed to requires reinforcement and behaves as complex contagion — so the same structural property has opposite signs for error and repair.
Centola & Macy (2007).
The reflection problem
Observed similarity within a group confounds three things: your behaviour responding to peers' behaviour, your behaviour responding to peers' characteristics, and you and your peers behaving alike because you share traits or an environment. In observational data these are not separately identified.
Manski (1993). Shalizi & Thomas (2011) prove the homophily– contagion version is generically confounded — a structural result, not a data problem. In agent systems ties and traits are independently assignable, which is what makes the confound a toggle.
